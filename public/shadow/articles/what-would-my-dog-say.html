What would my dog say if they could talk?

You know the look. You stand up, and your dog is already standing. You open the fridge, and there they are, sitting very nicely, as if they just happened to be passing. If your dog could talk, you would never get a quiet minute again.

They can't, so here is the next best thing: the everyday things dogs do, put into the words they'd probably use, with the plain, well-known reasons behind each habit. Spoiler: most of it comes back to you.

Why does my dog follow me to the bathroom?

What they'd say: "You went into a small room and closed the door. What if something exciting happens in there and I miss it?"

The usual reason: Dogs are social animals, and many like to stay close to their person. You are also where the good things come from: food, walks, attention. Following you is often just a dog keeping track of the best thing in the house.

Why does my dog tilt their head when I talk?

What they'd say: "Say 'walk' again. Slowly. I want to be sure."

The usual reason: Nobody knows for certain, but a common idea is that dogs tilt their heads to hear better or to catch a word they recognise. Many dogs learn their name and a handful of favourite words, so the tilt often shows up right when you say one.

Why does my dog steal my socks?

What they'd say: "They smell like you. You left them on the floor. I assumed they were a gift."

The usual reason: Your things carry your scent, which dogs tend to find comforting and interesting. A stolen sock also usually starts a very fun game of chase, so plenty of dogs learn it's a reliable way to get your attention.

Why does my dog stare at me while I eat?

What they'd say: "I'm not begging. I'm supervising. The difference is in the face."

The usual reason: Your dinner smells amazing to a dog, whose nose is far more powerful than ours. And if a bite has ever come their way, they remember. Staring costs nothing, and sometimes it works.

Why does my dog get the zoomies?

What they'd say: "I have no explanation. I had to go fast, and now I have gone fast."

The usual reason: Those sudden laps of the living room are usually a dog letting off built-up energy or excitement. They often show up after a bath, after a walk, or in the evening. Short, happy zoomies are generally a normal part of being a dog.

Why does my dog bring me a toy when I get home?

What they'd say: "You were gone for eleven years. Here is my best toy. Please don't leave again until we play."

The usual reason: For many dogs, a toy is part of the hello. They're excited, they want to play, and sometimes they just need something to carry while all that happiness happens.

Why does my dog lean on me?

What they'd say: "This is a hug. I don't have arms for the other kind."

The usual reason: Leaning is usually a way of being close to you. It often happens when a dog is feeling affectionate or wants a little reassurance, and bigger dogs especially like to put their whole weight into it.

Why does my dog spin before lying down?

What they'd say: "The bed was wrong. I fixed the bed. Now I may rest."

The usual reason: Circling is often thought to be an old instinct for flattening out a comfy spot, and it's usually nothing to think twice about. If your dog suddenly struggles to settle or seems sore lying down, check with a vet.

Why does my dog sigh so loudly?

What they'd say: "I am lying down. Everyone should know that I am lying down."

The usual reason: A big sigh as a dog settles is often just contentment, a lot like the noise you make sinking into the couch after a long day.

Can dogs understand what we say?

Many dogs learn to recognise certain words, like their name, "walk" or "treat", and most are very good at reading our tone of voice and body language. Full sentences are another story, which is where a little imagination comes in.
