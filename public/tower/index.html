Pear Lake is out. The hillsides are just getting started.
The fire burned 165,909 ha and took more than 120 structures. A preliminary post-wildfire hazard report, released Nov 9, says what comes next: floods, debris flows and landslides. The Clinton Creek watershed burned 86%, and it is the village's drinking water. Also on the list: Highways 97 and 99, the CN mainline and the BC Hydro Kelly Lake substation. A site-level assessment follows.

Nine fires out of control in November. Here is what that means.
Less than it sounds like. The last weekly national report, Nov 3, counted 129 active fires. Only 48 were getting a full response: 11 out of control, 6 held, 31 under control. Another 35 were on modified response and 46 were simply being monitored. Roughly two thirds of "active" is fires someone is watching, not fighting. We could not get a province-by-province split this week, so we are not going to guess.

Knowles Fire: the report is out
Four federal wildland firefighters died on June 27 in western Colorado when the larger Snyder Fire overtook crews on initial attack. They were Emily Barker, Nick Hutcherson, Nathan Matthews and Sydney Watson. A helitack crew member was injured.
Investigators do not point to one failure. They point to six things stacking up: extraordinary fire behaviour, strained communications, incomplete situational awareness, jurisdictional complexity, gaps in aerial supervision and extreme operational tempo. Read it before your next briefing.

Global fire emissions ran 20 to 31% below average, mostly because African savannas had a quiet year. The catch is where it did burn. Exposure was above average in 52 countries. The UK logged its first megafire. South Korea had its deadliest fires on record.
